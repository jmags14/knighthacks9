## Heat stroke is an emergency
Heat stroke can kill. Signs include a body temperature above 103 degrees F, hot red skin that may be dry or damp, a fast strong pulse, throbbing headache, confusion, slurred speech, dizziness, nausea, fainting, or passing out. Call 911 or get the person to a hospital right away if you can. While waiting, move them somewhere cooler and cool them with wet cloths or a cool bath. Do not give them anything to drink.
Source: National Weather Service - Heat-related illness <https://www.weather.gov/safety/heat-illness>

## Heat exhaustion
Heat exhaustion can come before heat stroke. Signs include heavy sweating, cool pale clammy skin, a fast weak pulse, muscle cramps, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea or vomiting, and fainting. Move the person to a cooler place, loosen their clothing, put cool wet cloths on them or have them sit in a cool bath, and give small sips of water. Get medical help if they vomit, if symptoms get worse, or if symptoms last more than 1 hour.
Source: National Weather Service - Heat-related illness <https://www.weather.gov/safety/heat-illness>

## Heat cramps
Heat cramps are painful muscle spasms, usually in the legs or stomach, with heavy sweating. They are often an early warning sign. Firmly press on or massage the muscle and give sips of water unless the person feels sick to their stomach. Get medical help if cramps last more than 1 hour.
Source: National Weather Service - Heat-related illness <https://www.weather.gov/safety/heat-illness>

## Staying cool when the power is out
Move to the coolest room, stay in the shade, and avoid hard physical work, especially in the hottest part of the day. Wear light, loose, light-colored clothing and wet your skin. Drink water regularly; pale yellow or clear urine usually means you are drinking enough. Limit sugary, salty, caffeinated, and alcoholic drinks. Fans help less in extreme heat: the CDC advises against relying on a fan when indoor temperatures are above 90 degrees F. If it is safe to travel, go somewhere with air conditioning, such as a cooling center. Calling 2-1-1 can help you find one.
Source: CDC - About heat and your health <https://www.cdc.gov/heat-health/about/index.html>; Ready.gov - Power Outages <https://www.ready.gov/power-outages>

## Who is most at risk from heat
Babies and young children, adults 65 and older, pregnant women, people with heart disease or asthma, people with long-term health problems, people who live alone, and anyone working outside are most at risk. Check on family and neighbors, especially people who live alone, and check on pets.
Source: CDC - About heat and your health <https://www.cdc.gov/heat-health/about/index.html>

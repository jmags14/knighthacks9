## Severe bleeding: when it is life-threatening
Bleeding is life-threatening if blood is spurting or flowing without stopping, if the person has lost a lot of blood (roughly half a soda can in an adult, less for a child), or if they show signs of shock. Call 911 right away if phones work. StormBox gives basic first aid information only; it is not a substitute for medical care or a first aid class.
Source: American Red Cross - Life-threatening external bleeding <https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/bleeding-life-threatening-external>

## How to stop severe bleeding
Find where the blood is coming from and cover the wound with a clean cloth or dressing. Press directly on the wound, firmly and steadily. If the wound is on an arm or leg, rest it on a firm, flat surface while you press. Keep pressing until the bleeding stops or help takes over. If blood soaks through, put another pad on top without removing the first one. A tourniquet can be used on an arm or leg for life-threatening bleeding by someone trained to use it. Protect yourself with gloves or a plastic bag if you can, or have the injured person press with their own hand.
Source: American Red Cross - Life-threatening external bleeding <https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/bleeding-life-threatening-external>

## After the bleeding stops
Once bleeding stops, check the skin beyond the wound for warmth and normal color, and ask whether the person feels numbness or tingling. Then wrap a bandage over the dressing to hold it in place. You can stop pressing if someone else takes over, if you are too exhausted to continue, or if the area becomes unsafe.
Source: American Red Cross - Life-threatening external bleeding <https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/bleeding-life-threatening-external>

## Small cuts and wounds after a storm
Injuries are common during and after hurricanes, especially during cleanup. Clean small cuts quickly with clean water and soap and cover them to prevent infection. Wounds that touched flood water are at higher risk of infection. If you get a puncture or a dirty wound, a doctor may need to know when you last had a tetanus shot. Watch for redness, swelling, or pus, which can mean infection.

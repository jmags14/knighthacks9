## Water coming into the house
If water is entering your home or a flood warning is in effect, act right away. Move people and pets to the highest floor you can reach. Do not touch electrical equipment that is wet, and never touch anything electrical while you are standing in water. Turn off the electricity at the main breaker only if you can reach it without standing in water. If you are trapped in a building, go to the highest level. Do not climb into a closed attic, because rising water can trap you there. Go onto the roof only if you must, and signal for help once you are there.
Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) - Floods <https://www.ready.gov/floods>

## Never walk or drive through flood water
Moving water is far stronger than it looks. About 6 inches of fast-moving water can knock an adult off their feet. About 12 inches of rushing water can carry away most cars, and about 2 feet can carry away SUVs and trucks. The CDC reports that more than half of flood drownings happen when a vehicle is driven into hazardous flood water. If you come to a flooded road or a barricade, turn around and find another way. Stay off bridges over fast-moving water, which can wash away without warning.
Source: National Weather Service - Turn Around Don't Drown <https://www.weather.gov/safety/flood-turn-around-dont-drown>

## Trapped in a car in moving water
If your car is caught in fast-moving water, stay inside the vehicle. If water begins rising inside the car, get onto the roof and call or signal for help. Do not try to walk or swim through moving water to reach safety.
Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) - Floods <https://www.ready.gov/floods>

## Flood water is dangerous to touch
Flood water can carry sewage, chemicals, sharp debris, and trash. It can also be electrified by downed or underground power lines, even if you cannot see a line. Snakes and other animals may be in the water or inside your home. Keep children out of flood water. If you touch flood water, wash your hands with soap and clean water, or use hand sanitizer if you have no clean water. Throw away any food that touched flood water.
Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) - Floods <https://www.ready.gov/floods>; CDC - Flood safety <https://www.cdc.gov/floods/safety/index.html>

## Cleaning up after a flood
Wait until local officials say it is safe before returning to a flooded home. Wear heavy work gloves, rubber boots, and protective clothing, and use a mask around mold or debris. Children should not help with flood cleanup. People with asthma, other lung problems, or weak immune systems should stay out of buildings with visible or smelly mold. Throw away soaked items that cannot be disinfected, such as rugs, cloth, and drywall. Clean hard surfaces with soap and water, then disinfect with 1 cup of bleach per gallon of water.
Source: Ready.gov (FEMA) - Floods <https://www.ready.gov/floods>; CDC - Flood safety <https://www.cdc.gov/floods/safety/index.html>

## Tetanus and wounds after a flood
Cuts and puncture wounds are common during flood cleanup, and flood water can carry germs into a wound. Clean small wounds quickly. Know when your last tetanus shot was, so you can tell a doctor if you get a puncture or a wound that touched dirty water.
Source: CDC - Flood safety <https://www.cdc.gov/floods/safety/index.html>
